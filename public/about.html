About this site

This site was made by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, working in Claude Code at the direction of Mark Graham (Internet Archive). Mark decided what it should be and what it should cover; Claude did the research, wrote the code that finds, pictures and labels the sites, and put it live on the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine Labs.

What it is

People around the world, often working with AI coding tools, are publishing small websites, explorers and web apps faster than ever. Many live on free hosting and disappear within months. The Wayback Machine's job is to keep a copy. This explorer is a pilot: it finds new sites as they are announced, saves each one in the Wayback Machine, and checks back to measure how long they last.

How sites are found

  • Hacker News. Every "Show HN" post, where people show something they made, except links to big sites such as GitHub or YouTube; and any other story that links to a site on a hosting platform.
  • Bluesky. Every new public post is read as it is published, and links to sites on hosting platforms are kept. Only the link and the time are kept, nothing about who posted it.
  • GitHub. The public GH Archive record of GitHub activity: new personal sites (name.github.io) and links to hosted sites in new projects' descriptions and release notes.
  • Hugging Face. Every new public Space (a small hosted web app, often an AI demo), from Hugging Face's own list of Spaces by creation date. A Space is kept once it is running: it is looked at again from two hours after it is created, for up to two days, and Spaces that never start are left out. The address saved is the app itself (name.hf.space).

"Hosting platforms" are about 50 services where small sites are published, such as GitHub Pages, Vercel, Netlify, Cloudflare Pages, Hugging Face Spaces, Replit, Lovable, Fly.io and Render.

What happens to each site

  1. About an hour after it is found, the site is checked; if it answers, a browser takes a picture of it and reads its title, description and text.
  2. Its address is sent to the Wayback Machine's Save Page Now. "Wayback copy" on a card links to that capture; "All Wayback captures" lists any others.
  3. The site is checked again 1, 7, 30, 60 and 90 days after it was found. "Gone" means the address no longer exists or the host shows its "nothing here" page; "Moved" means it now sends visitors to another address, often the owner's own domain; "Not answering" may be temporary.

How the labels are made

  • Built with comes from the page itself: tell-tale file names, page markers and the browser globals that frameworks such as Next.js, React, Vue, Astro and Svelte leave behind, the "generator" tag that site builders write, and the badges and file addresses of AI app builders such as Lovable, Bolt, v0 and Replit. A site built with a tool that leaves no trace has no label.
  • Topic comes from keyword rules on the title, description and text. They are simple and explainable, and wrong on some sites; a site can have up to three topics, or none.
  • Language is what the page says about itself; many pages do not say.
  • Unfinished starter pages, still showing a framework's default title such as "Vite + React", are hidden unless you ask for them.

Flagged sites

Pages that look like adult content, gambling, scams or phishing (for example a page asking for a bank or wallet login) are saved in the Wayback Machine like any other, but are left out of the main view. To see them, choose Flagged sites in the filters. They are shown on their own, with their pictures blurred until you choose to see each one. Pages flagged as phishing have no link to the live site, only to the Wayback Machine copy, because they are built to trick people into typing passwords. Flagged sites are not in the sitemap.

The flags are automatic: they come from words on the page (in English, and for adult pages also in Japanese, Chinese and Korean) and will miss some pages and catch some wrongly. If you see something that should be flagged or should not be, or you made a site listed here and want it removed from the explorer, write to info@archive.org.

Web archives welcome

Web archives are welcome to capture this site. If your crawler has trouble capturing it, contact info@archive.org. Every view has its own address, and the sitemap lists the main ones.

Accessibility

We aim for this site to meet the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2 at level AA: it works with a keyboard and a screen reader, every filter has a label, text and controls have enough contrast in light and dark mode, and the layout works on a phone. The pictures of sites are screenshots; each card's title and address say what it shows. If something does not work for you, please tell us at info@archive.org.
